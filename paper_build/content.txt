FRONTH ABSTRACT
PN Dental screening at CodiSmile Dental Clinic relies on visual inspection, which makes early tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation hard to detect consistently, leaves no standardized visual record, and gives patients little finding-specific guidance. This study developed Toothwise, a web-based screening support system that detects these four conditions and healthy tooth in still intraoral photographs with a fused YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 ensemble, computes a 0–100 risk score and a low, medium, or high risk tier, and generates a personalized prevention plan with a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. The system was developed with an Agile methodology using React, FastAPI, and PostgreSQL, and deployed on cloud hosting. On a held-out test set of 2,930 images, YOLOv8 reached an mAP@50 of 51.91% and YOLOv12 48.11%, and the fused ensemble 54.24%; cavity was detected best and plaque worst. The KNN classifier reproduced the clinical risk rule with 92.75% accuracy on 2,000 unseen cases, and all 119 automated backend tests passed. The system was evaluated through an online questionnaire answered by 103 respondents (98 patient respondents and 5 IT experts) on a four-point scale. The six ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics (security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection), rated by 12 respondents, obtained an overall weighted mean of 3.68, and the system's detection, results, and prevention-plan features, rated by all 103, obtained 3.48; both are interpreted as Highly Acceptable. The study concludes that Toothwise is an acceptable screening and documentation aid that supports, but does not replace, the dentist's diagnosis, and recommends more training data for weak classes such as plaque, the use of an intraoral camera for clearer images, a larger evaluation with dentists and clinic staff, and a controlled comparison of screening time and consistency.
PN ***Keywords:*** dental screening, YOLOv8, YOLOv12, ensemble object detection, K-Nearest Neighbors, prevention plan, ISO/IEC 25010

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem
P Dental screening at CodiSmile Dental Clinic relies on visual inspection. Early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation are small, look similar to one another, and vary with lighting, angle, and stage, so visual assessment is subjective and findings can differ between examinations. The clinic has no standardized visual record of findings to compare across visits, and patients are rarely given finding-specific preventive guidance. Undetected conditions can progress to pain, tooth loss, and costlier treatment, which makes consistent early screening a challenge of the method itself.
P This study seeks to answer the following:
1. What limitations of visual-only screening make early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation and healthy tooth, as well as their relative severity, difficult to detect and distinguish consistently at CodiSmile Dental Clinic?
2. What gaps exist in how screening findings are recorded and made accessible to dentists and patients, given the absence of a standardized visual record and a way for patients to review their own history?
3. What barriers prevent patients from receiving timely, finding-specific preventive guidance after screening?
4. What is the level of acceptability of the Toothwise system in terms of the six ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics evaluated in this study (security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection), as evaluated by IT experts and patient respondents?
5. How do the respondents assess the effect of Toothwise on screening consistency and screening time compared with the current manual process?

## 2.1 Related Literature
P **Convolutional Neural Networks for Dental Image Diagnostics.** Schwendicke et al. (2019) carried out a scoping review of 36 studies, published from 2015 to 2019, that applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to dental images. Most of the studies detected, segmented, or classified anatomical structures such as teeth, jaw bone, and skeletal landmarks, and only a few targeted pathologies such as caries. The authors noted that CNNs have shown the potential to detect structures and pathologies on medical images with accuracy similar to, or even higher than, that of professionals, but they also warned of possible bias in the underlying algorithms and of limited robustness and generalizability. These cautions carry directly into Toothwise, where the models are evaluated on a held-out test split and all detected findings at CodiSmile Dental Clinic are reviewed by a licensed dentist before being communicated to any patient. The review also shows that the field has concentrated on detection and segmentation tasks, which is the gap that the personalized prevention plan feature of Toothwise was built to address.

## 3.2 Project Planning
PN The Toothwise project was planned around the screening and record-keeping workflow of CodiSmile Dental Clinic. The following considerations guided the planning of the system:
- **Project Objectives** — to provide CodiSmile Dental Clinic with an AI-assisted dental screening system that detects tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth from a still photograph, estimates a risk level, and generates a recommendation/prevention plan, while also supporting day-to-day clinic record-keeping.
- **Project Scope** — the system covers staff authentication, patient management, dental screening and AI detection from uploaded still images, risk assessment, recommendation generation, password recovery, clinical notes, PDF reporting, audit logging, a self-service patient portal, a public AI assistant, and cloud deployment.
- **Target Users** — CodiSmile Dental Clinic staff (dentist, receptionist, and assistant roles) and the clinic's patients through the patient portal.
- **Clinic Workflow** — a patient is registered or already on file, a still intraoral photograph taken with a standard device camera is uploaded during a screening, the system produces detection, risk, and recommendation results for the dentist to review, and the dentist may confirm the risk tier, add clinical notes, or generate a PDF report.
- **Functional Requirements** — detailed in Section 3.4.
- **Non-Functional Requirements** — detailed in Section 3.4, covering security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection.
- **AI Screening Requirements** — the system was planned to use two independent detection models (YOLOv8 and YOLOv12) whose outputs are fused into a single clinical detection set, rather than relying on a single model.
- **Database Requirements** — a relational PostgreSQL schema was planned to store staff accounts, patients, screenings and their photographs, detections, recommendations, clinical notes, and audit logs with referential integrity between related records.
- **Security Requirements** — JWT-based authentication, hashed passwords, rate-limited login endpoints, secure password recovery, authenticated (non-public) image access, and audit logging were planned from the outset given the clinical and personal nature of the data involved.
- **Deployment Requirements** — the system had to run on free cloud hosting without a payment card, which limits the backend to 512 MB of memory and a disk that is cleared whenever the service restarts.

### 3.10.4 Statistical Treatment
P The weighted mean of each statement is computed as WM = Σ(f × x) / N, where f is the number of respondents who chose a rating, x is the rating (1 to 4), and N is the number of respondents who answered the statement. The mean of a section is the average of its statement means, and the overall mean is the average of the section means. The standard deviation (SD) of the ratings shows how closely the respondents agreed with one another: the smaller the SD, the more uniform the ratings.
### 3.10.5 Ethical Considerations

The questionnaire asked for a name only as an optional field; names are not reported anywhere in this paper, and results are presented only as group statistics. No patient records or photographs from CodiSmile Dental Clinic were collected for the evaluation, and all data were handled in accordance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). The screening images shown in this paper are demonstration images.

### 4.1.3 Overall Detection Performance of YOLOv8 and YOLOv12
P Five standard measures are used to score the models. **Precision** is the share of the model's marks that are correct: of all the boxes it drew, how many were real findings. **Recall** is the share of real findings that the model found: of all the conditions in the photographs, how many it marked. The **F1-score** combines precision and recall into one number. **mAP@50** (mean average precision) is the main overall score: it counts a finding as found when the model's box overlaps the correct box by at least half, and averages the result over the five conditions. **mAP@50-95** is the same score with stricter overlap rules, so it is always lower. For all five measures, higher is better, and 100% would be perfect.
P Table {tab:overall} gives the scores on two sets of photographs: the validation set (5,299 images), scored at the end of training, and the test set (2,930 images), which neither model had seen before. The test-set scores are the fairest comparison.
TABLE overall | Overall Detection Performance of YOLOv8 and YOLOv12

| Metric | YOLOv8 (validation) | YOLOv12 (validation) | YOLOv8 (test) | YOLOv12 (test) |
| Precision | 53.96% | 49.43% | 54.32% | 52.94% |
| Recall | 49.73% | 49.05% | 52.34% | 50.06% |
| F1-score | 51.76% | 49.24% | 53.31% | 51.46% |

P YOLOv8 scored a little higher than YOLOv12 on every measure. On the test set, its mAP@50 was 51.91% against 48.11% for YOLOv12, its precision 54.32% against 52.94%, and its recall 52.34% against 50.06%. In simple terms, each model correctly found about half of the conditions in the test photographs. ### 4.1.5 Detection Accuracy and Confusion Matrix
P Detection has no single built-in accuracy score, because a model can go wrong in three ways: it can give a finding the wrong name, mark a spot where there is nothing (a false alarm), or miss a finding completely. In this study, **detection accuracy** is the number of findings that were found and named correctly, divided by all four outcomes together (correct, wrong name, false alarm, and missed). The counts were taken at the same confidence setting that the live system uses (0.25). Tables {tab:acc} and {tab:accclass} give the results, and Figure {fig:cm} shows the confusion matrices, which show how often each condition was mistaken for another.

Giving a finding the wrong name was rare, so when a model does find a condition, it usually names it correctly. The most common mix-up between two conditions was tartar being called a cavity.
P The combined result had slightly lower accuracy than YOLOv8 even though its mAP@50 was higher (Section 4.1.7). The reason is that it keeps a finding when either model reports it. It therefore missed fewer findings (2,216 against 2,874) but raised more false alarms (3,574 against 1,992). Detection accuracy counts both kinds of mistake equally, while mAP rewards placing correct findings ahead of wrong ones.
P Two cautions apply. First, the four photo collections did not all mark the same conditions; for example, a photograph from a collection without a gum-inflammation label may show inflamed gums that nobody marked. When the model correctly finds them, the count treats it as a false alarm, so the real accuracy is probably somewhat higher. Second, the counts depend on the confidence setting: a lower setting finds more but also raises more false alarms.
P For a screening tool, a missed finding is the more serious mistake: a false alarm is simply checked and dismissed by the dentist, but a missed finding might not be looked at. This is why Toothwise presents its detections as suggestions and keeps the dentist's own examination as the final word.

### 4.1.7 Ensemble Fusion
P In the live system, both models look at every photograph and their results are combined into one set of findings. When the two models mark the same condition in about the same place (their boxes overlap by at least half), the two boxes are merged into one. A finding that both models agree on is given a slightly higher confidence (plus 0.08, up to 0.98), because two separately trained models agreeing is stronger evidence than one model alone.

### 4.1.8 Summary of the Model Comparison
P To answer the first problem: both models learned to find the five conditions. YOLOv8 was slightly more accurate (mAP@50 of 51.91% against 48.11%), while YOLOv12 was smaller and slightly faster (about 206 ms against 216 ms per photograph). The difference between the two models is small compared with the difference between conditions: cavity was found reliably, but plaque was hard for both. Combining the models gave the best result (mAP@50 of 54.24%, detection accuracy 43.02%). These results confirm what Chapter I states: Toothwise is a screening aid, not a replacement for the dentist. Its findings are suggestions that the dentist confirms or corrects.

## 4.2 Specialization and Technical Results
P This section describes how the finished system works and how well it performed: the screening steps, the risk score, the prevention plan, the patient records, user roles and security, the chatbot, and the speed and accessibility of the website.
### 4.2.1 AI Detection Pipeline
P When staff upload a photograph of a patient's teeth, the system goes through six steps: (1) it checks that the file is a JPEG, PNG, or WebP image of at most 15 MB; (2) it runs YOLOv8 and YOLOv12; (3) it combines their findings (Section 4.1.7) and removes braces brackets that the models mistake for findings; (4) it computes a risk score and risk level; (5) it writes a prevention plan; and (6) it saves the photograph, every finding with its box and confidence, the risk result, and the plan in the patient's record. The live system also keeps a copy of each photograph in the database, because the free server erases its own files whenever it restarts; the copy is used to restore the photograph for viewing and for the PDF report.
P Neither model learned what braces look like, so in early testing the metal brackets on teeth with braces were reported as urgent cavities. The system now ignores any cavity, plaque, or tartar box in which 8% or more of the pixels are metal-grey, reports a cavity only when its confidence is at least 0.50, and keeps a cavity only when both models find it. When two or more brackets are seen, the prevention plan adds a note that braces were detected and that a dentist or orthodontist should check those teeth in person. These rules were added after the accuracy tests in Section 4.1, so Tables {tab:acc}, {tab:accclass}, and {tab:fusion} show the results before the rules.
### 4.2.2 Risk Scoring Engine
P The risk score follows a fixed rule, so that every score can be explained to the patient and checked by the dentist. Each condition has a severity weight, cavity being the most serious (Table {tab:risk}). For each finding, the weight is multiplied by the model's confidence; the score is the highest of these values, plus 6 points for each additional finding (up to five more), with a maximum of 100. The score then decides the risk level: LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH.

| Healthy tooth | 0 | 0 (no risk contribution) |
| Plaque | 25 | 25 (LOW) |
| Tartar | 35 | 35 (MEDIUM) |
| Gum inflammation | 45 | 45 (MEDIUM) |
| Cavity | 70 | 70 (HIGH) |
ENDTABLE

### 4.2.3 KNN-Based Recommendation Feature
P The prevention plan uses a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. In simple terms, KNN looks at the five past cases that are most similar to the current screening and uses them to decide its risk level; the plan is then written from that level and the specific conditions found. Because there were no dentist-checked cases yet, the classifier first learned from made-up (synthetic) cases created with the risk rule. Each time a dentist confirms or corrects a risk level in the system, that case is saved as a real example for future retraining.
P Table {tab:knn} shows how the classifier did on 2,000 new made-up cases that it had not seen before. Since these cases follow the same rule it learned from, this test shows how well it copies the rule, not how accurate it is on real patients.

No dentist-checked cases were recorded during the study, because the clinic's patients were not screened, so the classifier's accuracy on real cases could not yet be measured; this is recommended in Chapter V.
### 4.2.4 Results Interface and Patient Records
P To answer the second problem: every screening is now saved as a standard visual record. It contains the original photograph, colored boxes showing each finding with its name and confidence, a plain-language explanation of each condition, the risk score and level, the prevention plan, and the dentist's notes. The patient record lists all screenings by date with a risk trend chart, so visits can be compared, and each screening can be downloaded as a PDF report (Figure {fig:s_report}). Figures {fig:s_detail}, {fig:s_result}, and {fig:s_phome} to {fig:s_report} in Section 3.11 show these screens. Patients can also create their own portal account, where they see their latest risk level and how it changed since their last visit, their latest findings explained simply with the top next steps, a risk-over-time chart, the date of their next check-up (six months after the last screening), and their full screening history, but never another patient's data.
### 4.2.5 Role-Based Web Application
P The staff side has three roles. The dentist is the administrator: the dentist can manage staff accounts and view the audit log. The receptionist registers and manages patients, and the dental assistant performs screenings. Patients have a separate login. The server checks each user's role on every request, not just by hiding menu buttons. Passwords are stored in scrambled (hashed) form, sign-ins use secure tokens, repeated login and chatbot requests are limited, and important staff actions are recorded in an audit log to which records are only added, never changed.
P Staff and patients sign in with their email and password. Ticking “Remember me on this device” keeps them signed in for 30 days; otherwise they are signed out when the browser closes. A user who forgets the password can reset it without email: staff confirm their email and the PRC license number on their account, and patients confirm their email, birthdate, and mobile number, and then choose a new password on the same page. A wrong answer always shows the same message, so nobody can use the form to find out which accounts exist; attempts are limited to five per minute, and every attempt is recorded in the audit log. An earlier version emailed a reset link, but the free server could not send email reliably, so that step was removed. Staff with no license number on file ask the dentist to reset their password. Signed-in users can also change their password from the sidebar or the portal header.
P The system's server code is checked by 119 automated tests. They cover signing in, password recovery, the braces filter, the chatbot, staff accounts, the condition list, the detection models, patient records, the prevention plan, the risk score, user roles, and screenings, including restoring photographs from the database. #CH CHAPTER V | SUMMARY OF FINDINGS, CONCLUSIONS, AND RECOMMENDATIONS
P This chapter summarizes the findings of the study, states the conclusions drawn from them, and presents recommendations for CodiSmile Dental Clinic, for the further development of Toothwise, and for future researchers.
## 5.1 Summary of Findings
P The findings are summarized according to the five problems stated in Chapter I.
1. **Detection of the five conditions (Problem 1).** YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 were trained on 40,271 images merged from four public datasets and tested on 2,930 unseen images. On the test split, YOLOv8 obtained a precision of 54.32%, a recall of 52.34%, and an mAP@50 of 51.91%; YOLOv12 obtained 52.94%, 50.06%, and 48.11%, with about 24% fewer parameters and slightly faster inference (about 206 ms against 216 ms per image on CPU). Fusing the two models raised the mAP@50 to 54.24%. Healthy tooth and cavity were detected best, and plaque worst (mAP@50 below 10%). Detection accuracy at the deployed threshold was 43.77% for YOLOv8, 41.85% for YOLOv12, and 43.02% for the ensemble. In the deployed system, a screening takes about 3 to 4 seconds, and metal brackets are filtered out so that they are not reported as cavities. The respondents who evaluated the current manual process agreed that it has the limitations described in the first problem (mean 3.21).
2. **Recording and access to findings (Problem 2).** Every screening is saved as a standardized visual record with the photograph, labeled bounding boxes, the risk result, the prevention plan, and the dentist's notes; it can be compared across visits, exported as a PDF report, and viewed by the patient through the self-service portal, which now also shows the latest results, a risk-over-time chart, and the next check-up date. Respondents rated the results interface and patient records 3.47, Strongly Agree.
3. **Finding-specific preventive guidance (Problem 3).** The rule-based risk engine and the KNN classifier (K = 5) produce a risk tier and an ordered prevention plan for every screening. On 2,000 unseen synthetic cases, the classifier reproduced the clinical rule with 92.75% accuracy; its weakest tier was HIGH (recall 58.18%). Respondents rated the prevention plan 3.50, Strongly Agree, the highest of the three feature sections.
4. **Acceptability (Problem 4).** On the six quality characteristics, answered by 12 respondents, the overall mean was 3.68 (Highly Acceptable), with Privacy highest (3.72) and Reliability lowest (3.61). The three feature sections, answered by all 103 respondents, obtained an overall mean of 3.48 (Highly Acceptable). IT experts rated every section lower than patient respondents, but still Strongly Agree. Technically, the web application scored 100 in Lighthouse accessibility and best practices on all audited pages and passed all 119 automated backend tests.
5. **Screening consistency and time (Problem 5).** A controlled, timed comparison against the dentist's findings was not conducted. The 2 respondents who answered the related section agreed that Toothwise makes screening more consistent and faster to document (mean 3.40), and the measured processing time of the deployed system is about 3 to 4 seconds per screening.
## 5.2 Conclusions
P Based on the findings, the following conclusions are drawn.
1. YOLO-based detection on ordinary intraoral photographs can locate and label tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth, but with an mAP@50 of about 52% to 54% on unseen images and large differences between conditions, it is suitable as a screening aid that directs the dentist's attention, not as an independent diagnosis. This confirms the delimitation set in Chapter I.
2. The difference between YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 is small compared with the differences between conditions, so the quality, balance, and consistency of the training data matter more than the choice of architecture. Combining both models improved detection over either model alone, which supports the ensemble design.
3. Storing each screening as a standardized visual record, with patient portal access, addresses the absence of comparable records across visits identified in the second problem.
4. Finding-specific prevention plans can be generated automatically for every screening without adding to the dentist's work, and the respondents found them relevant and easy to follow.
5. Toothwise is highly acceptable to its evaluators on all six quality characteristics and on its main features (overall means of 3.68 and 3.48 on a four-point scale). Because the quality characteristics were intended for dentists, clinic staff, and IT experts and were rated by 12 respondents, of whom 5 were IT experts, this conclusion should be confirmed with a larger group of IT experts, dentists, and clinic staff.
6. Toothwise shortens the documentation of a screening, since results, risk, and the prevention plan are produced and recorded in seconds, and the respondents expect it to make screening more consistent. Whether it improves agreement with the dentist and shortens the whole screening in practice could not be established without a controlled comparison.
## 5.3 Recommendations
P In light of the findings and conclusions, the following are recommended.
PN **For CodiSmile Dental Clinic**
1. Use Toothwise as a screening and documentation aid, always with the dentist's review of the detections and confirmation of the risk tier, and take clear, well-lit photographs, since blurry images reduce accuracy.
2. Confirm or correct the risk tier of every screening so that the KNN classifier is retrained on real, dentist-labeled cases.
3. Move the system from the free hosting tier to a plan that does not sleep, so that the first page load is not delayed, before using it in daily operations.
PN **For the further development of Toothwise**
1. Improve detection of plaque and other weak classes by adding more and better-annotated photographs, including consented photographs from the clinic, and add a braces class instead of filtering brackets.
2. Add the features requested by respondents: reminders for brushing and check-ups, more dental health tips and short videos, a faster and simpler search, and lighter pages for slow Internet connections.
3. Add a second authentication factor for staff accounts, as one respondent suggested, and keep the audit and privacy controls aligned with the Data Privacy Act of 2012.
4. Simplify pages that present much information at once and make less-used features easier to find.
PN **For future researchers**
1. Conduct the controlled comparison planned for the fifth problem: time the same screenings with and without Toothwise and measure agreement with the dentist's findings (for example, with Cohen's kappa).
2. Repeat the ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation with a larger and more balanced group of IT experts, dentists, and clinic staff, the groups for which the quality evaluation was designed.
3. Validate the models on photographs from other clinics and devices, and compare them with newer detection architectures and with segmentation-based approaches.
4. Train the detection models on more and larger datasets. The current models learned from four public datasets in which plaque appears in only one source, which explains much of the weak plaque result. More public datasets, consented and dentist-annotated clinic photographs, and a more balanced number of examples per condition should raise accuracy.
5. Add an oral diagnosis form to the system: a digital examination form in which the dentist records the clinical findings for each tooth (for example, on a tooth chart), the final diagnosis, and the treatment plan, next to the AI detections. The recorded diagnoses could then be compared with the detections to measure accuracy in real use and supply labeled cases for retraining.
6. Extend the system to detect more classes. The source datasets already contain conditions that this study left out, such as missing teeth, malalignment, spacing, tooth stains and discoloration, oral ulcers, and hypodontia, and orthodontic braces could be added as their own class instead of being filtered out. Each new class needs enough annotated examples, a risk weight, and its own advice.
7. Use an intraoral camera to capture the images for detection. Smartphone photographs vary in lighting and angle and cannot clearly show the back teeth; an intraoral camera gives close, well-lit, and consistent views of each tooth. Future researchers could connect one to the screening page and retrain the models on its images.
