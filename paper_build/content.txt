FRONTH ABSTRACT
PN Dental screening at CodiSmile Dental Clinic relies on visual inspection, which makes early tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation hard to detect consistently, leaves no standardized visual record, and gives patients little finding-specific guidance. This study developed Toothwise, a web-based screening support system that detects these four conditions and healthy tooth in still intraoral photographs with a fused YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 ensemble, computes a 0–100 risk score and a low, medium, or high risk tier, and generates a personalized prevention plan with a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. The system was developed with an Agile methodology using React, FastAPI, and PostgreSQL, and deployed on cloud hosting. On 2,081 duplicate-free test images, YOLOv8, YOLOv12, and their ensemble each exceeded 80% on all six image-level measures for any dental condition and for cavity; box-level mAP@50 was about 50%. The KNN classifier reproduced the clinical risk rule with 92.75% accuracy on 2,000 unseen cases, and all 118 automated backend tests passed. The system was evaluated through an online questionnaire answered by 103 respondents (98 patient respondents and 5 IT experts) on a four-point scale. The six ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics (security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection), rated by 12 respondents, obtained an overall weighted mean of 3.68, and the system's detection, results, and prevention-plan features, rated by all 103, obtained 3.48; both are interpreted as Highly Acceptable. The study concludes that Toothwise is an acceptable screening and documentation aid that supports, but does not replace, the dentist's diagnosis, and recommends more training data for weak classes such as plaque, the use of an intraoral camera for clearer images, a larger evaluation with dentists and clinic staff, and a controlled comparison of screening time and consistency.
PN ***Keywords:*** dental screening, YOLOv8, YOLOv12, ensemble object detection, K-Nearest Neighbors, prevention plan, ISO/IEC 25010

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem
P Dental screening at CodiSmile Dental Clinic relies on visual inspection. Early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation are small, look similar to one another, and vary with lighting, angle, and stage, so visual assessment is subjective and findings can differ between examinations. The clinic has no standardized visual record of findings to compare across visits, and patients are rarely given finding-specific preventive guidance. Undetected conditions can progress to pain, tooth loss, and costlier treatment, which makes consistent early screening a challenge of the method itself. Any tool introduced to address this must also be fast and dependable enough not to slow down the clinic's daily work.
P Specifically, this study addresses the following problems:
1. Early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation, as well as healthy teeth, are difficult to detect and distinguish consistently through visual-only screening, and the clinic has no objective, measured means of identifying these conditions in intraoral photographs or of estimating a patient's risk level from them.
2. Screening findings are not kept in a standardized visual record, so dentists cannot readily compare findings across visits, and patients have no way to review their own screening results and history.
3. Patients rarely receive timely, finding-specific preventive guidance after screening; the advice they receive is often generic rather than based on their own findings and risk level.
4. The acceptability of Toothwise in terms of the six ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics evaluated in this study (security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection) has not yet been established among IT experts and patient respondents.
5. It has not yet been established whether an AI-assisted screening system such as Toothwise can complete a screening quickly and run reliably enough for daily use in a clinic.

## 2.1 Related Literature
P **Convolutional Neural Networks for Dental Image Diagnostics.** Schwendicke et al. (2019) carried out a scoping review of 36 studies, published from 2015 to 2019, that applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to dental images. Most of the studies detected, segmented, or classified anatomical structures such as teeth, jaw bone, and skeletal landmarks, and only a few targeted pathologies such as caries. The authors noted that CNNs have shown the potential to detect structures and pathologies on medical images with accuracy similar to, or even higher than, that of professionals, but they also warned of possible bias in the underlying algorithms and of limited robustness and generalizability. These cautions carry directly into Toothwise, where the models are evaluated on a held-out test split and all detected findings at CodiSmile Dental Clinic are reviewed by a licensed dentist before being communicated to any patient. The review also shows that the field has concentrated on detection and segmentation tasks, which is the gap that the personalized prevention plan feature of Toothwise was built to address.

## 3.2 Project Planning
PN The Toothwise project was planned around the screening and record-keeping workflow of CodiSmile Dental Clinic. The following considerations guided the planning of the system:
- **Project Objectives** — to provide CodiSmile Dental Clinic with an AI-assisted dental screening system that detects tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth from a still photograph, estimates a risk level, and generates a recommendation/prevention plan, while also supporting day-to-day clinic record-keeping.
- **Project Scope** — the system covers staff authentication, patient management, dental screening and AI detection from uploaded still images, risk assessment, recommendation generation, password recovery, clinical notes, PDF reporting, audit logging, a self-service patient portal, a public AI assistant, and cloud deployment.
- **Target Users** — CodiSmile Dental Clinic staff (dentist, receptionist, and assistant roles) and the clinic's patients through the patient portal.
- **Clinic Workflow** — a patient is registered or already on file, a still intraoral photograph taken with a standard device camera is uploaded during a screening, the system produces detection, risk, and recommendation results for the dentist to review, and the dentist may confirm the risk tier, add clinical notes, or generate a PDF report.
- **Functional Requirements** — detailed in Section 3.4.
- **Non-Functional Requirements** — detailed in Section 3.4, covering security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection.
- **AI Screening Requirements** — the system was planned to use two independent detection models (YOLOv8 and YOLOv12) whose outputs are fused into a single clinical detection set, rather than relying on a single model.
- **Database Requirements** — a relational PostgreSQL schema was planned to store staff accounts, patients, screenings and their photographs, detections, recommendations, clinical notes, and audit logs with referential integrity between related records.
- **Security Requirements** — JWT-based authentication, hashed passwords, rate-limited login endpoints, secure password recovery, authenticated (non-public) image access, and audit logging were planned from the outset given the clinical and personal nature of the data involved.
- **Deployment Requirements** — the system had to run on free cloud hosting without a payment card, which limits the backend to 512 MB of memory and a disk that is cleared whenever the service restarts.

## 3.10 Evaluation Procedure
P This section describes how the acceptability of Toothwise was evaluated (the fourth problem of the study, with supporting information for the fifth): who the respondents were, the instrument they answered, how the data were gathered, and how the responses were treated. The results are presented in Sections 4.3 and 4.4.
### 3.10.1 Respondents and Data Gathering
P The evaluation questionnaire was prepared in Google Forms and shared online. Participation was voluntary: the form opened with a statement explaining the purpose of the study and the handling of the data, and only respondents who agreed to it could continue. Each respondent first stated their group, either patient respondent (a member of the public evaluating the system from a patient's point of view) or IT expert (IT practitioner), and then answered the parts of the questionnaire intended for them. The software quality part (ISO/IEC 25010) was written for dentists, clinic staff, and IT experts, while the parts on the system's features were written for all respondents. Responses were collected from October 19 to 28, 2026, and 103 complete submissions were received.
P The study used non-probability sampling, since there was no fixed list of the people who could evaluate the system from which a random sample could be drawn. Patient respondents were reached through convenience sampling: the link to the form was shared online, for example on social media and in group chats, and anyone who received it and agreed to take part could answer. IT experts were chosen through purposive sampling: the researchers personally invited people who work in information technology, because the software quality part of the questionnaire calls for technical knowledge (Etikan et al., 2016). Because the population was not fixed, no sample-size formula was applied, and since the respondents were not randomly selected, the results describe the respondents of this study and are not generalized to all patients or all IT practitioners. Tables {tab:resp} and {tab:sectn} show how many respondents answered each section.

### 3.10.4 Statistical Treatment
P The weighted mean of each statement is computed as WM = Σ(f × x) / N, where f is the number of respondents who chose a rating, x is the rating (1 to 4), and N is the number of respondents who answered the statement. The mean of a section is the average of its statement means, and the overall mean is the average of the section means. The standard deviation (SD) of the ratings shows how closely the respondents agreed with one another: the smaller the SD, the more uniform the ratings.
### 3.10.5 Ethical Considerations
P All respondents gave their informed consent before answering. The questionnaire asked for a name only as an optional field; names are not reported anywhere in this paper, and results are presented only as group statistics. No patient records or photographs from CodiSmile Dental Clinic were collected for the evaluation, and all data were handled in accordance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). The screening images shown in this paper are demonstration images.

## 4.1 Results of the Trained Datasets
P This section answers the first problem: how well can the trained models tell whether a photograph of the teeth shows tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, or a healthy tooth, and which model, YOLOv8 or YOLOv12, does it better? The main results are scored per photograph (Sections 4.1.4 and 4.1.5). The standard YOLO scores follow as supporting results (Section 4.1.6).
### 4.1.1 Dataset Preparation
P The models learned from four free, public collections of dental photographs in which the conditions had already been marked by hand. Each collection named its conditions differently, so every condition was matched to one of the five Toothwise classes, and conditions outside the study (for example, missing teeth, crooked teeth, gaps, ulcers, and missing tooth buds) were left out. Some collections outlined each condition as a shape instead of a box, so those outlines were turned into boxes, which is the format both models use. Table {tab:remap} shows how each collection's conditions were matched.

### 4.1.3 How the Models Were Scored
P The main scores are given per photograph. For each photograph and each condition, two things are compared: does the photograph show the condition, and did the model report it? This gives four possible outcomes:
- **True positive (TP):** the photograph shows the condition and the model reported it (a correct find).
- **False positive (FP):** the photograph does not show the condition, but the model reported it (a false alarm).
- **False negative (FN):** the photograph shows the condition, but the model did not report it (a missed finding).
- **True negative (TN):** the photograph does not show the condition and the model did not report it (correctly left unmarked).
P Six scores are calculated from these four counts:
- **Accuracy:** how many photographs were judged correctly.
- **Precision:** when the model says a condition is there, how often it is right.
- **Recall:** of the photographs that show the condition, how many the model found.
- **Specificity:** of the photographs without the condition, how many the model correctly left unmarked.
- **F1-score:** one number that combines precision and recall.
- **AUROC:** how well the model's confidence separates photographs with the condition from photographs without it, without depending on a cut-off.
P **Any finding** asks whether the photograph shows at least one of the four conditions (cavity, gum inflammation, plaque, or tartar). This is the main question a screening tool answers. A model reports a condition when its confidence reaches a cut-off. For each condition, the cut-off that gave the best F1-score on the validation set was chosen and then used unchanged on the test set. The live system currently uses fixed cut-offs (0.25, and 0.50 for cavity), and results at those cut-offs are also shown.
P The standard YOLO score, mAP@50, is stricter: it counts a finding only when the model's box overlaps the hand-drawn box by at least half. It is given as a supporting result, because the job of Toothwise is to tell the dentist whether a condition is present, not to draw its exact outline.

### 4.1.8 Combining the Two Models
P In the live system, both models look at every photograph and their findings are combined. When both models mark the same condition in about the same place, the two marks become one, and the finding gets a slightly higher confidence (plus 0.08, up to 0.98), because two models agreeing is stronger evidence than one.
P The combined models found the most problems: for any finding, their recall was 88.0% against 85.5% for YOLOv8, with the best F1-score (87.4%) and AUROC (90.7%). They also had the highest AUROC of the three for every condition (Table {tab:imgcond} and Appendix D). The cost is slightly more false alarms (specificity 81.5% against 83.9% for YOLOv8). Since a missed finding matters more in screening than a false alarm that the dentist can dismiss, this supports running both models.
### 4.1.9 Summary of the Model Comparison
P To answer the first problem: on 2,081 clean test photographs, YOLOv8, YOLOv12, and the combined models all scored above 80% on all six per-photograph measures for finding any dental problem and for finding cavities. Tartar came close, while gum inflammation and plaque were weak because of too few and inconsistently marked training photographs. YOLOv8 was slightly better than YOLOv12, YOLOv12 was smaller and slightly faster, and combining them found the most problems. On the stricter standard YOLO score, both reached about 50% mAP@50. Toothwise is therefore a screening aid, not a replacement for the dentist: its findings are suggestions that the dentist confirms or corrects.

## 4.2 Specialization and Technical Results
P This section describes how the finished system works and how well it performed: the screening steps, the risk score, the prevention plan, the patient records, user roles and security, and the chatbot.
### 4.2.1 AI Detection Pipeline
P When staff upload a photograph of a patient's teeth, the system goes through six steps: (1) it checks that the file is a HEIC/HEIF, JPEG, PNG, or WebP image of at most 15 MB, and converts HEIC photographs, the default camera format of iPhones, to a full-resolution JPEG; (2) it runs YOLOv8 and YOLOv12; (3) it combines their findings (Section 4.1.8) and removes braces brackets that the models mistake for findings; (4) it computes a risk score and risk level; (5) it writes a prevention plan; and (6) it saves the photograph, every finding with its box and confidence, the risk result, and the plan in the patient's record. The live system also keeps a copy of each photograph in the database, because the free server erases its own files whenever it restarts; the copy is used to restore the photograph for viewing and for the PDF report.
P Neither model learned what braces look like, so in early testing the metal brackets on teeth with braces were reported as urgent cavities. The system now ignores any cavity, plaque, or tartar box in which 8% or more of the pixels are metal-grey, reports a cavity only when its confidence is at least 0.50, and keeps a cavity only when both models find it. When two or more brackets are seen, the prevention plan adds a note that braces were detected and that a dentist or orthodontist should check those teeth in person. The results in Section 4.1 do not include the braces filter; the cavity rules are included only in Table {tab:imgdep}.
### 4.2.2 Risk Scoring Engine
P The risk score follows a fixed rule, so that every score can be explained to the patient and checked by the dentist. Each condition has a severity weight, cavity being the most serious (Table {tab:risk}). For each finding, the weight is multiplied by the model's confidence; the score is the highest of these values, plus 6 points for each additional finding (up to five more), with a maximum of 100. The score then decides the risk level: LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH.

| Plaque | 25 | 25 (LOW) |

### 4.2.3 KNN-Based Recommendation Feature
P The prevention plan uses a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier. In simple terms, KNN looks at the five past cases that are most similar to the current screening and uses them to decide its risk level; the plan is then written from that level and the specific conditions found. Because there were no dentist-checked cases yet, the classifier first learned from made-up (synthetic) cases created with the risk rule. Each time a dentist confirms or corrects a risk level in the system, that case is saved as a real example for future retraining.

### 4.2.4 Results Interface and Patient Records
P To answer the second problem: every screening is now saved as a standard visual record. It contains the original photograph, colored boxes showing each finding with its name and confidence, a plain-language explanation of each condition, the risk score and level, the prevention plan, and the dentist's notes. The patient record lists all screenings by date with a risk trend chart, so visits can be compared, and each screening can be downloaded as a PDF report (Figure {fig:s_report}). Below every result, an image-level evaluation of the screening shows how far its findings can be trusted (Figure {fig:s_rel}): for each condition, whether the model being viewed found it in this photograph, with its highest confidence and number of marks, next to that model's measured per-photograph scores from Section 4.1 at the cut-offs the system uses. A found condition with an F1-score below 60%, such as gum inflammation or plaque, is flagged to be confirmed by examination, and a photograph with no findings carries a reminder that the models miss about a quarter of problem photographs. Because the dentist's own findings are not recorded per condition, this panel shows the expected reliability of each result, not whether that particular result is correct. Figures {fig:s_detail}, {fig:s_result}, and {fig:s_phome} to {fig:s_report} in Section 3.11 show these screens. Patients can also create their own portal account, where they see their latest risk level and how it changed since their last visit, their latest findings explained simply with the top next steps, a risk-over-time chart, the date of their next check-up (six months after the last screening), and their full screening history, but never another patient's data.
### 4.2.5 Role-Based Web Application
P The staff side has three roles. The dentist is the administrator: the dentist can manage staff accounts and view the audit log. The receptionist registers and manages patients, and the dental assistant performs screenings. Patients have a separate login. The server checks each user's role on every request, not just by hiding menu buttons. Passwords are stored in scrambled (hashed) form, sign-ins use secure tokens, repeated login and chatbot requests are limited, and important staff actions are recorded in an audit log to which records are only added, never changed.
P Staff and patients sign in with their email and password. Ticking “Remember me on this device” keeps them signed in for 30 days; otherwise they are signed out when the browser closes. A patient who forgets the password can reset it without email by confirming their email, birthdate, and mobile number, and then choosing a new password on the same page. A wrong answer always shows the same message, so nobody can use the form to find out which accounts exist; attempts are limited to five per minute, and every attempt is recorded in the audit log. Staff passwords are reset only by the dentist, from the Staff page, so a staff account cannot be taken over through the reset form. An earlier version emailed a reset link, but the free server could not send email reliably, so that step was removed. Before anyone can use the system, a Data Privacy Notice explains what data are collected and why, how they are protected, that AI results are not a diagnosis, and the user's rights under the Data Privacy Act of 2012; the user must tick the agreement to continue, once per browser session, so each person on a shared clinic computer sees it (Figure {fig:s_privacy}). Signed-in users can also change their password from the sidebar or the portal header.
### 4.2.6 AI Chatbot
P The public website has an AI assistant, powered by Google Gemini, that answers general questions about dental care, the clinic, and Toothwise. Before a question is sent to Gemini, email addresses, phone numbers, and record numbers are removed, and any request for personal records or screening results is pointed to the secure patient portal instead. Each user can send up to 12 questions per minute. When the free Gemini quota runs out, questions about the clinic (hours, location, services, and prices) are still answered from the clinic's verified information, and other questions get a “temporarily unavailable” message.

### 4.4.1 Screening Speed
P On an ordinary computer without a graphics card, each model analyzed a photograph in about one fifth of a second, and both models together in about half a second (Table {tab:speed}). The only noticeable delay is the first request after the free server has been idle for 15 minutes, which can take about a minute while the service starts again (Section 1.4); a paid hosting plan that does not sleep removes this delay.

#CH CHAPTER V | SUMMARY OF FINDINGS, CONCLUSIONS, AND RECOMMENDATIONS
P This chapter summarizes the findings of the study, states the conclusions drawn from them, and presents recommendations for CodiSmile Dental Clinic, for the further development of Toothwise, and for future researchers.
## 5.1 Summary of Findings
P The findings are summarized according to the five problems stated in Chapter I.
1. **Detection of the five conditions (Problem 1).** YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 were trained on 40,271 images merged from four public datasets. A duplicate check found that 849 of the 2,930 test images also appeared, as copies or near-copies, in the training or validation split; these were removed, and the models were evaluated on the remaining 2,081 clean test images. In the primary, image-level evaluation with validation-tuned thresholds, YOLOv8, YOLOv12, and the fused ensemble each exceeded 80% in accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and AUROC in identifying images that show a dental condition (F1-scores of 86.7%, 86.3%, and 87.4%; AUROC 90.0%, 89.8%, and 90.7%) and images that show a cavity (F1-scores of 86.4%, 85.5%, and 86.3%). At the cut-offs the live system currently uses, precision rose to about 90% to 93% but recall fell to 71.9% to 78.0%, so the validation-tuned cut-offs are recommended. Tartar reached an F1-score of 73.7% to 76.0%, gum inflammation 41.0% to 59.3%, and plaque 22.6% to 31.1%. In the secondary, box-level evaluation on the clean split, YOLOv8 obtained an mAP@50 of 49.99% and YOLOv12 49.81%. YOLOv12 has about 24% fewer parameters and was slightly faster (about 206 ms against 216 ms per image on CPU), and the fused ensemble had the highest recall, F1-score, and AUROC. In the deployed system, a screening takes about 3 to 4 seconds, and metal brackets are filtered out so that they are not reported as cavities. The respondents who evaluated the current manual process agreed that it has the limitations described in the first problem (mean 3.21).
2. **Recording and access to findings (Problem 2).** Every screening is saved as a standardized visual record with the photograph, labeled bounding boxes, the risk result, the prevention plan, and the dentist's notes; it can be compared across visits, exported as a PDF report, and viewed by the patient through the self-service portal, which now also shows the latest results, a risk-over-time chart, and the next check-up date. Respondents rated the results interface and patient records 3.47, Strongly Agree.
3. **Finding-specific preventive guidance (Problem 3).** The rule-based risk engine and the KNN classifier (K = 5) produce a risk tier and an ordered prevention plan for every screening. On 2,000 unseen synthetic cases, the classifier reproduced the clinical rule with 92.75% accuracy; its weakest tier was HIGH (recall 58.18%). Respondents rated the prevention plan 3.50, Strongly Agree, the highest of the three feature sections.
4. **Acceptability (Problem 4).** On the six quality characteristics, answered by 12 respondents, the overall mean was 3.68 (Highly Acceptable), with Privacy highest (3.72) and Reliability lowest (3.61). The three feature sections, answered by all 103 respondents, obtained an overall mean of 3.48 (Highly Acceptable). IT experts rated every section lower than patient respondents, but still Strongly Agree.
5. **Speed and reliability (Problem 5).** In the deployed system, a full screening with both models takes about 3 to 4 seconds, and a model analyzes a photograph in about 0.2 seconds on an ordinary computer. All 118 automated backend tests passed. The website scored 100 in Lighthouse accessibility and best practices on all audited pages, and 99 to 100 for speed on desktop and 86 to 98 on phones. Respondents rated Performance Efficiency 3.67 and Reliability 3.61 (Highly Acceptable). A timed comparison of screening consistency and time against the manual process was not conducted.
## 5.2 Conclusions
P Based on the findings, the following conclusions are drawn.
1. YOLO-based detection on ordinary intraoral photographs reliably identifies whether a photograph shows a dental condition, and in particular a cavity, with all image-level measures above 80% on unseen, duplicate-free images. It is less reliable for tartar and gum inflammation, unreliable for plaque, and places its boxes exactly on only about half of the hand-marked findings (mAP@50 of about 50%). It is therefore suitable as a screening aid that directs the dentist's attention, not as an independent diagnosis. This confirms the delimitation set in Chapter I.
2. The difference between YOLOv8 and YOLOv12 is small compared with the differences between conditions, so the quality, balance, and consistency of the training data matter more than the choice of architecture. Combining both models found more conditions than either model alone, which supports the ensemble design. Checking the test set for duplicates of training images is necessary when public datasets are merged: about a quarter of the original test images were such duplicates.
3. Storing each screening as a standardized visual record, with patient portal access, addresses the absence of comparable records across visits identified in the second problem.
4. Finding-specific prevention plans can be generated automatically for every screening without adding to the dentist's work, and the respondents found them relevant and easy to follow.
5. Toothwise is highly acceptable to its evaluators on all six quality characteristics and on its main features (overall means of 3.68 and 3.48 on a four-point scale). Because the quality characteristics were intended for dentists, clinic staff, and IT experts and were rated by 12 respondents, of whom 5 were IT experts, this conclusion should be confirmed with a larger group of IT experts, dentists, and clinic staff.
6. Toothwise is fast and reliable enough for daily clinic use: it completes a screening in seconds, passed all of its automated tests, and its website is fully accessible. The free hosting tier is the only obstacle, because its first request after a quiet period is slow. Whether Toothwise also makes screening more consistent than the manual process could not be established without a controlled comparison.
## 5.3 Recommendations
P In light of the findings and conclusions, the following are recommended.
PN **For CodiSmile Dental Clinic**
1. Use Toothwise as a screening and documentation aid, always with the dentist's review of the detections and confirmation of the risk tier, and take clear, well-lit photographs, since blurry images reduce accuracy.
2. Confirm or correct the risk tier of every screening so that the KNN classifier is retrained on real, dentist-labeled cases.
3. Move the system from the free hosting tier to a plan that does not sleep, so that the first page load is not delayed, before using it in daily operations.
PN **For the further development of Toothwise**
1. Improve detection of plaque and other weak classes by adding more and better-annotated photographs, including consented photographs from the clinic, and add a braces class instead of filtering brackets. Before retraining, split the data by original photograph so that copies of one photograph never fall into different splits, and give each photograph one consistent set of labels.
2. Switch the live system to the validation-tuned confidence thresholds reported in Chapter IV, which find more conditions than the current fixed thresholds, after confirming them on clinic photographs.
3. Add the features requested by respondents: reminders for brushing and check-ups, more dental health tips and short videos, a faster and simpler search, and lighter pages for slow Internet connections.
4. Add a second authentication factor for staff accounts, as one respondent suggested, and keep the audit and privacy controls aligned with the Data Privacy Act of 2012.
5. Simplify pages that present much information at once and make less-used features easier to find.
PN **For future researchers**
1. Conduct a controlled comparison of screening consistency and time: time the same screenings with and without Toothwise and measure agreement with the dentist's findings (for example, with Cohen's kappa).
2. Repeat the ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation with a larger and more balanced group of IT experts, dentists, and clinic staff, the groups for which the quality evaluation was designed.
3. Validate the models on photographs from other clinics and devices, and compare them with newer detection architectures and with segmentation-based approaches.
4. Train the detection models on more and larger datasets. The current models learned from four public datasets in which plaque appears in only one source, which explains much of the weak plaque result. More public datasets, consented and dentist-annotated clinic photographs, and a more balanced number of examples per condition should raise accuracy.
5. Add an oral diagnosis form to the system: a digital examination form in which the dentist records the clinical findings for each tooth (for example, on a tooth chart), the final diagnosis, and the treatment plan, next to the AI detections. The recorded diagnoses could then be compared with the detections to measure accuracy in real use and supply labeled cases for retraining.
6. Extend the system to detect more classes. The source datasets already contain conditions that this study left out, such as missing teeth, malalignment, spacing, tooth stains and discoloration, oral ulcers, and hypodontia, and orthodontic braces could be added as their own class instead of being filtered out. Each new class needs enough annotated examples, a risk weight, and its own advice.
7. Use an intraoral camera to capture the images for detection. Smartphone photographs vary in lighting and angle and cannot clearly show the back teeth; an intraoral camera gives close, well-lit, and consistent views of each tooth. Future researchers could connect one to the screening page and retrain the models on its images.
