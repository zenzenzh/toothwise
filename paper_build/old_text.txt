H CHAPTER I
H INTRODUCTION
H 1.1 Background of the Study
P CodiSmile Dental Clinic currently relies on manual visual examination and professional judgment to identify dental caries and other visible dental conditions from patient examinations and intraoral images. Although this approach is essential to dental practice, manually reviewing images may be time-consuming and can result in variations in assessment and documentation. To support the existing process, the proponents propose Toothwise: A YOLO-Based Multi-Condition Dental Screening System with KNN-Based Recommendation for CodiSmile Dental Clinic, an AI-assisted system that analyzes intraoral images and detects possible dental conditions — tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth — using the YOLOv12 and YOLOv8 object detection models. The system provides visual detection results, condition labels, descriptions, a computed risk classification, and preventive recommendations to assist dentists and improve patient understanding. Toothwise is intended to serve as a supplementary screening and documentation tool, while final interpretation and clinical decisions remain the responsibility of licensed dental professionals.
P Dental caries, commonly referred to as tooth decay or cavities, remains one of the most prevalent non-communicable diseases in the world. The World Health Organization (2024) estimates that oral diseases affect nearly 3.7 billion people globally, with untreated dental caries in permanent teeth identified as the single most widespread health condition based on Global Burden of Disease 2021 data. Despite its prevalence, a significant portion of the population, particularly those in geographically isolated or economically disadvantaged communities, continues to face substantial barriers in accessing timely and adequate dental care. The financial cost of consultations, the distance to dental facilities, and low oral health literacy frequently result in delayed care-seeking, by which point cavities have often advanced beyond their most treatable stages. According to Qari et al. (2023), untreated dental caries does not just cause physical pain, it also affects a person's confidence and overall quality of life.
P A critical dimension of the current dental care challenge lies in the inconsistency of manual cavity screening practices. Wang et al. (2026), in a review on the clinical translation of AI-based caries diagnosis, noted that traditional diagnostic methods suffer from limited sensitivity and high variability between examiners, particularly for early-stage lesions. This inter-examiner variability is particularly problematic in high-volume clinic settings where practitioner fatigue and time pressure can further compromise screening reliability. Research has shown that deep learning models are capable of detecting dental cavities with accuracy that is on par with, or sometimes even better than, trained clinicians, as confirmed by systematic reviews from Schwendicke et al. (2021) and Mohammad Rahimi et al. (2022). This makes AI a genuinely useful tool for supplementing human judgment during screenings.
P Standard digital cameras and smartphone cameras have also changed the way dental screening can be supported outside specialized clinical equipment. Instead of relying purely on what a dentist can see with a mirror and a light, or requiring dedicated intraoral camera hardware, clear intraoral photographs can now be captured using widely available consumer devices. Studies such as those by Moharrami et al. (2025) and Askar et al. (2021) show that RGB photographs captured under ordinary imaging conditions are detailed enough to support reliable artificial intelligence-based detection of dental conditions. It is this still-image, standard-camera modality that serves as the primary data source for Toothwise during patient screenings at CodiSmile Dental Clinic, since the system is designed to work from uploaded or device-captured photographs rather than requiring a dedicated intraoral camera.
P The AI model powering the primary detection module of this system is YOLOv12, which was introduced by Tian et al. (2025) as an attention-centric object detection framework. Compared to previous versions in the YOLO family, YOLOv12 uses area attention mechanisms that help it focus selectively on spatially relevant regions, something that happens regularly in dental images where carious lesions appear as small, low-contrast areas across multiple tooth surfaces simultaneously. Its combination of speed and accuracy makes it well-suited for efficient use during clinical consultations, where screening results need to be generated quickly enough to fit within a standard patient visit.
P To evaluate whether YOLOv12's architectural improvements translate into meaningful gains for this specific screening task, this study also trains and evaluates YOLOv8, an earlier and widely adopted model in the YOLO family (Ultralytics, 2023). YOLOv8 has been applied in several prior dental imaging studies with strong results; de Brito et al. (2025) used YOLOv8 together with EfficientNet-B0 to diagnose caries in interproximal radiographic images of young permanent molars, and other work has applied YOLOv8's anchor-free detection mechanism to interproximal caries detection with improved accuracy and speed over earlier YOLO versions. This makes it a stable, well-benchmarked baseline for object detection tasks. While YOLOv12 and YOLOv8 are formally compared against each other using precision, recall, mAP, and inference speed on a held-out labeled test set, both models are also deployed together in the operational system, where their independent detection outputs are fused into a single clinical detection set. This dual approach allows the study to identify which architecture performs better on this specific task while still taking advantage of both models' detections in the deployed screening workflow.
P Beyond detection, Toothwise also generates a personalized prevention plan for each patient based on their screening results. This is done using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm, a supervised learning method originally introduced by Cover and Hart (1967), which classifies a new case by comparing it against the most similar cases in a reference dataset. In this study, KNN classifies each screening into a risk tier (low, medium, or high) based on the patient's detected findings — condition type, number of affected areas, and severity indicators derived from the detection output — and this risk classification, together with condition-specific rules, is used to compose an ordered, patient-friendly prevention plan, while the 0–100 risk score itself comes from a separate rule-based formula. Since no dentist-labeled cases exist before deployment, the KNN is first trained on synthetic cases derived from the clinical severity rule, and each tier the dentist confirms or corrects is added as a real case for retraining.
P While AI-assisted dental screening has grown internationally, its application in the Philippine setting remains an emerging area of research. Wu et al. (2025), in collaboration with Ateneo de Manila University's Laboratory for Intelligent Visual Environments, applied a deep learning model to identify tooth and sinus structures in dental panoramic radiographs, illustrating the early but growing interest in AI-assisted dental diagnostics locally. This study seeks to build on that emerging interest by applying AI-assisted screening directly within a clinic setting through Toothwise.
P This project was created in partnership with CodiSmile Dental Clinic, which serves as the deployment site and clinical reference point throughout the study. The goal is not to take the place of the dentist but to give them a more efficient and objective tool to work with. With Toothwise integrated into the clinic's workflow, screenings can be documented more consistently, and patients can walk away with a clearer understanding of their oral health through the system's risk classification and personalized prevention plan features.
H 1.2 Statement of the Problem
P Dental screening at CodiSmile Dental Clinic relies on visual inspection. Early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation are small, look similar to one another, and vary with lighting, angle, and stage, so visual assessment is subjective and findings can differ between examinations. The clinic has no standardized visual record of findings to compare across visits, and patients are rarely given finding-specific preventive guidance. Undetected conditions can progress to pain, tooth loss, and costlier treatment, which makes consistent early screening a challenge of the method itself. This study seeks to answer the following: 1. What limitations of visual-only screening make early-stage tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation and healthy tooth, as well as their relative severity, difficult to detect and distinguish consistently at CodiSmile Dental Clinic? 2. What gaps exist in how screening findings are recorded and made accessible to dentists and patients, given the absence of a standardized visual record and a way for patients to review their own history? 3. What barriers prevent patients from receiving timely, finding-specific preventive guidance after screening? 4. What is the level of acceptability of the Toothwise system in terms of the six quality characteristics of ISO/IEC 25010 (security, privacy, usability, reliability, performance efficiency, and data protection), as evaluated by IT experts and clinical end users? 5. What is the difference on the use of Toothwise to improve screening consistency and reduce screening time compared with the current manual process?

H 1.4 Scope, Delimitation and Limitation of the Study
H Scope
P This study covers the design, development, training, and evaluation of Toothwise, an AI-assisted dental screening support system for CodiSmile Dental Clinic. Using still images captured through a standard device camera, such as a smartphone or webcam, and uploaded to the system, Toothwise analyzes the patient's teeth and gums to detect five classes: tartar, plaque, cavity, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth. YOLOv12 and YOLOv8 models are trained on publicly available annotated dental image datasets and compared in terms of precision, recall, mean average precision (mAP), and inference speed; both models are also used together in the deployed system, with their outputs fused into a single clinical detection set. After training, the system is tested at CodiSmile Dental Clinic to assess how it performs in an actual clinical environment. Detection results are displayed through a results interface that shows bounding boxes, condition labels, plain-language descriptions, and a computed risk classification for licensed dentists, and findings are saved in a per-patient record that can be reviewed across visits. Based on the detected findings, a recommendation feature that uses the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm classifies the screening into a risk tier and generates a personalized prevention plan for each patient. The system also generates a downloadable PDF screening report for clinical recordkeeping and maintains an audit log of sensitive staff actions for administrative review. The intended users are licensed dentists and clinic personnel at CodiSmile Dental Clinic; in addition, patients may register for a self-service portal to view their own screening history and past findings. The system is evaluated by IT experts and clinical end users using the six quality characteristics of ISO/IEC 25010, and its effect on screening consistency and screening time is compared with the current manual process, using the dentist's findings as the reference.
H Delimitations
P Toothwise is a support tool and not a replacement for a licensed dentist. The dentist at CodiSmile Dental Clinic always has the final say on any diagnosis or treatment decision. The prevention plan is meant to guide the patient and is not a formal medical prescription, so it does not include medication, treatment procedures, or dosages. The system works only with still photographic images captured through a standard device camera or uploaded from an existing file, and does not process X-rays or any other radiographic input, nor does it require specialized intraoral camera hardware. It is limited to the five classes named above and does not detect oral cancer, bone loss, impacted teeth, orthodontic issues, or other conditions that surface-level visual detection cannot reliably identify. The comparison of detection models is limited to YOLOv12 and YOLOv8 and does not include other detection architectures. The system is developed and tested only at CodiSmile Dental Clinic and does not integrate with billing or appointment scheduling systems. The evaluation covers software performance, quality, and acceptability, and does not measure long-term clinical outcomes such as changes in caries rates over time.
H Limitations
P The accuracy of the detection models depends on image quality, lighting, camera positioning, and the patient's cooperation during capture, and may vary more than it would with specialized intraoral camera hardware since a standard device camera is used instead. Because the models are trained on public datasets, performance in the clinic may differ from training results if the clinic's captured images look different from the dataset images. The quality of the KNN-generated risk classification and recommendations depends on its reference data, which starts as synthetic cases and grows as the dentist confirms tiers. Findings may not generalize to other clinics, camera devices, or patient populations.
H 1.5 Significance of the Study
PN This study is expected to benefit the following groups:
P **Patients.** Patients of CodiSmile Dental Clinic may benefit from a more consistent screening experience, in which findings such as tartar, plaque, cavities, gum inflammation, and healthy tooth are shown visually, explained in plain language, and accompanied by a risk classification. The personalized prevention plan gives them specific guidance based on their own findings, which can support earlier preventive action and better awareness of their oral health, and the patient portal allows them to review their own screening history at any time.
P **Dentists and CodiSmile Dental Clinic.** Toothwise serves as a screening support tool that gives licensed dentists a consistent, image-based reference during consultations, while diagnosis and treatment decisions remain with them. The per-patient record of images, findings, and risk classifications gives the clinic a standardized documentation of screenings that can be reviewed and compared across visits, which the clinic currently lacks, and the PDF reporting and audit logging features support clinical recordkeeping and administrative oversight.
P **IT Practitioners and Software Developers.** The comparison of YOLOv12 and YOLOv8, the use of KNN for risk classification and recommendations, and the ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation provide a documented example of building and assessing an AI-based health support system in a real clinic setting.
P **Future Researchers.** Students and researchers interested in AI in dentistry can use the methodology, dataset preparation, model comparison, and system design of this study as a basis for further work, such as expanding the detectable conditions, testing other clinics, or studying long-term clinical outcomes.
P **The Research Community.** There are relatively few local studies that apply AI-based detection to dental screening in the Philippine setting. This project contributes to that growing body of work and may serve as a reference for similar initiatives at other clinics across the country.
H 1.6 Definition of Terms
P For clarity and common understanding, the following terms are defined based on how they are used in this study.
P **Calculus (Tartar).** A hardened deposit of mineralized plaque that forms on the tooth surface and along the gumline. In this study, it refers to one of the five conditions detected by the Toothwise system.
P **Dental Caries (Cavity).** The decay and structural breakdown of tooth enamel and underlying tissue caused by bacterial activity. In this study, it refers to one of the five conditions detected by the Toothwise system.
P **Gum Inflammation (Gingival Inflammation).** A visible reddening, swelling, or irritation of the gum tissue, often an early indicator of gingivitis. In this study, it refers to one of the five conditions detected by the Toothwise system.
P **Inference Speed.** The amount of time a trained detection model takes to process an input image and produce a detection result. In this study, it is one of the metrics used to compare the performance of YOLOv12 and YOLOv8.
P **K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN).** A supervised machine learning algorithm introduced by Cover and Hart (1967), which classifies a new case by identifying and comparing it to the most similar cases in a reference dataset. In this study, KNN is used to classify each screening into a risk tier and generate a personalized prevention plan based on a patient's detected findings.
P **Mean Average Precision (mAP).** A standard evaluation metric for object detection models that summarizes a model's precision and recall performance across all detected classes. In this study, it is one of the metrics used to compare YOLOv12 and YOLOv8.
P **Plaque.** A soft, sticky film of bacteria that forms on the surface of teeth. In this study, it refers to one of the five conditions detected by the Toothwise system.
P **Precision.** A performance metric that measures the proportion of correctly identified detections out of all detections made by the model. In this study, it is used to evaluate and compare the performance of YOLOv12 and YOLOv8.
P **Recall.** A performance metric that measures the proportion of actual conditions in an image that were correctly identified by the model. In this study, it is used to evaluate and compare the performance of YOLOv12 and YOLOv8.
P **Risk Score.** A numerical value from 0 to 100, classified as low, medium, or high, generated by the system from a patient's detected findings to indicate the overall severity of a screening result. In this study, the risk score is computed by a rule-based formula.
P **Toothwise.** The AI-assisted dental screening support system developed in this study, which uses YOLOv12 and YOLOv8 for detection and KNN for risk classification and generating personalized prevention plans.
P **YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once, version 8).** An object detection model architecture in the YOLO family, used in this study as a baseline for comparison against YOLOv12 in detecting dental conditions.
P **YOLOv12 (You Only Look Once, version 12).** An attention-centric object detection model architecture introduced by Tian et al. (2025), used in this study as the primary detection model for identifying dental conditions from intraoral images.
H CHAPTER II
P **REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES** 
P This chapter presents the related literature and studies that have been gathered by the proponents. It introduces many ideas linked to the topic, which includes the research's principal focus. The study's synthesis is offered to analyze the previous research that the researchers have gathered.
H 2.1 Related Literature
P **Convolutional Neural Networks for Dental Image Diagnostics.** Schwendicke et al. (2021) put together a systematic review of how convolutional neural networks had been applied in dental imaging, covering everything from caries detection to tooth segmentation. What stood out from their findings was that CNN models were not just competitive with experienced dental clinicians but actually outperformed them in some cases. The authors were careful to point out, though, that the quality of training data matters enormously. Models trained on poorly labeled or inconsistent datasets tend to underperform in real clinical scenarios. They also made it clear that these tools are meant to assist dentists, not replace them. That design principle carries directly into Toothwise, where all detected findings at CodiSmile Dental Clinic are reviewed by a licensed dentist before being communicated to any patient. The review also noted the absence of systems that go beyond detection and actually provide clinical guidance afterward, which is precisely the gap that the personalized prevention plan feature of Toothwise was built to address. 
P **Deep Learning for Caries Detection: A Systematic Review.** Mohammad Rahimi et al. (2022) reviewed a large number of studies that used machine learning and deep learning to detect caries across different image types, including intraoral photographs. Their main takeaway was that deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks, worked reliably for identifying both early and more advanced carious lesions. They also raised a concern that is very relevant to this project: there are not many large, standardized, and publicly available dental datasets out there, which makes it harder to train models that perform well across different clinical settings. For Toothwise, this limitation was addressed by combining publicly available annotated datasets with data augmentation techniques during model training. The review also found that no existing study had combined automated caries detection with a recommendation output, which reinforces why the prevention plan feature of Toothwise fills a genuine gap in the existing body of work. 
P **Deep Learning for Detecting Dental Plaque and Gingivitis from Oral Photographs.** Moharrami et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review evaluating deep learning models specifically for detecting dental plaque and gingivitis from RGB intraoral photographs, including images captured through standard consumer devices rather than specialized dental equipment. Across the studies they reviewed, models showed stronger and more consistent performance for plaque detection than for gingivitis, where the certainty of evidence was rated lower due to the more subtle and gradual visual presentation of early gingival inflammation. This finding is directly relevant to Toothwise, since gum inflammation is one of the five conditions the system is trained to detect from standard-camera photographs; it signals that this class may require more careful dataset curation and potentially larger sample sizes than the other conditions to reach comparable performance during training. 
P **Oral Screening of Dental Calculus, Gingivitis, and Dental Caries through Segmentation on Intraoral Photographic Images.** Zhang (2024) developed a deep learning segmentation system, Oral-Mamba, capable of simultaneously detecting three conditions — dental caries, dental calculus, and gingivitis — from a single set of intraoral photographic images, achieving accuracy rates above 0.81 for each condition. This study is one of the few in the literature that approaches multi-condition detection from a single intraoral image source rather than treating each condition as a separate, isolated detection task. It directly supports the feasibility of the multi-class detection approach used in Toothwise, which similarly trains a single model to identify several distinct conditions — tartar, plaque, cavity, and gum inflammation — from one still-image source. 
P **Oral Health Philippines Country Profile.** The World Health Organization (2022) released a country-specific oral health profile for the Philippines as part of its Global Oral Health Status Report. The numbers are difficult to ignore. Dental caries was listed as one of the most prevalent health concerns in the country, with high rates of untreated cases across all age groups. The report also pointed out something that many Filipinos already know from experience: access to dental care is not evenly distributed. People in rural and community settings have significantly fewer options, and the limited dental workforce makes it harder to reach those who need care the most. This profile is the most authoritative reference for understanding the national oral health landscape that Toothwise seeks to contribute to. Deploying an artificial intelligence-assisted screening system at CodiSmile Dental Clinic is a concrete step toward addressing the access and consistency gaps that the WHO profile describes. 
P **Pediatrician Knowledge, Attitude and Practices on Children's Oral Health in the Philippines.** Oliveros Villarico et al. (2023) looked at something that often goes undiscussed, which is the role of pediatricians in preventing dental caries among Filipino children. Their study, conducted at a tertiary public hospital in Metro Manila, found that a significant number of pediatricians had limited awareness of evidence-based caries prevention approaches, even though the 2018 DOH National Survey on Oral Health already documented that 85.2% of Filipino five-year-olds had dental caries. The gap between what the data shows and what frontline healthcare workers actually know about it is striking. For this project, this literature helps explain why a tool like Toothwise matters at the clinic level. If even pediatricians are not fully equipped to address oral caries early, having an AI-assisted screening system that flags dental conditions during a regular consultation at CodiSmile Dental Clinic can make a real difference in catching problems before they get worse. 
P **Guiding Responsible Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare in the Philippines.** Alibudbud et al. (2025) wrote a narrative review and policy analysis on responsible AI adoption in Philippine healthcare. They went through the current landscape of AI applications in the country, examined what regulatory frameworks exist, and laid out recommendations for making sure AI tools in clinical settings are used ethically and safely. Some of the key concerns they raised were limited digital infrastructure, the lack of local clinical validation standards, and the need for healthcare workers to understand and critically evaluate AI outputs. They also emphasized that AI systems used in Philippine healthcare should be transparent, subject to human oversight, and compliant with the Data Privacy Act of 2012. Reading this study shaped several decisions in how Toothwise was designed, particularly the requirement that all AI detection results at CodiSmile Dental Clinic must be reviewed and confirmed by a licensed dentist before they are communicated to patients, and that all patient data is stored securely with authenticated access and audit logging. 
P **An Overview of Recommendation Techniques and Their Applications in Healthcare.** Yue et al. (2021) reviewed the major recommendation approaches used in healthcare contexts, namely content-based, collaborative filtering, and hybrid methods, and mapped their application across five scenarios: dietary recommendation, lifestyle recommendation, training recommendation, decision-making support for patients and physicians, and disease-related prediction. Their review positions K-Nearest Neighbors-based approaches as a foundational technique underlying several of these healthcare recommendation scenarios, particularly where recommendations are generated by classifying a case according to its similarity to previously seen cases. This framework directly informs how the KNN-based risk classification and prevention plan feature of Toothwise was conceptualized, positioning it within the "decision-making support for patients" category identified in their review, an application area that, per their analysis, remains comparatively underexplored relative to dietary and lifestyle recommendation systems. 
H 2.2 Related Studies
P **Detecting White Spot Lesions on Dental Photography Using Deep Learning.** Askar et al. (2021) worked on detecting white spot lesions in intraoral dental photos captured with standard consumer cameras, using deep learning. White spot lesions are early stage demineralization, essentially the very beginning of a cavity before it becomes a visible hole. What made this study particularly useful is that it was done with a small dataset, which is a realistic constraint for many research projects, including this one. Despite that limitation, the model still managed to detect subtle surface level changes that would be easy to miss in a routine visual check. For Toothwise, this matters because it shows that even with limited annotated data and images from standard, non-specialized cameras, deep learning can pick up early, crucial changes from photographs, which is the same image modality the system uses at CodiSmile Dental Clinic. It also gave the team confidence that training a YOLOv12 model for early cavity detection is achievable even when the training dataset is not massive and specialized imaging hardware is not required. 
P **Ultralytics YOLO (Version 8.0.0).** Jocher et al. (2023) released YOLOv8 as an open source framework under the Ultralytics platform. Compared to older YOLO versions, YOLOv8 came with a cleaner training pipeline, an anchor free detection head, and support for multiple tasks like detection, segmentation, and classification all in one package. It became one of the most widely used detection frameworks in both academic and applied settings because it was relatively easy to work with while still delivering strong results. Understanding YOLOv8 matters for this project because YOLOv12 was built on the same foundation. The training tools, dataset formats, and evaluation methods used in YOLOv8 largely carry over to YOLOv12, so much of what Jocher et al. (2023) established applies directly to how the Toothwise models were trained and tested. YOLOv8 also serves as the direct comparison baseline against YOLOv12 in this study's model evaluation. 
P **Accuracy in Diagnosing Caries in Young Permanent Molars Using Interproximal Radiographic Imaging and Validation by Artificial Intelligence.** de Brito et al. (2025) applied YOLOv8 together with a pre-trained EfficientNet-B0 model to diagnose caries in interproximal radiographic images of young permanent molars, comparing the AI-based evaluation against diagnoses made by dental professionals. Their results showed strong inter-examiner agreement and confirmed that YOLOv8 performs reliably even on the small, low-contrast lesion types that are typically difficult to detect visually. This study provides direct evidence that YOLOv8 is a credible, well-validated baseline for detecting caries-type conditions, supporting its selection as the comparison model against YOLOv12 in this study. 
P **Deep Learning in Oral Hygiene: Automated Dental Plaque Detection via YOLO Frameworks and Quantification Using the O'Leary Index.** Ramírez-Pedraza et al. (2025) trained and compared several YOLO model variants, including YOLOv11, specifically for detecting dental plaque in varied imaging conditions, using the O'Leary Index as a clinical benchmark for plaque quantification. Their results showed that YOLO-based models could reliably distinguish plaque at different stages of maturity, though newer, less mineralized plaque was harder to detect than older, more visually distinct plaque, since it tends to blend with surrounding gingival tissue. This finding is directly relevant to Toothwise, since it identifies a specific detection challenge, distinguishing early plaque from gum tissue, that the model must account for during training and dataset annotation for the plaque class. 
P **Diagnostic Accuracy of Dental Caries Detection Using Ensemble Deep Learning with Intraoral Camera Images.** Kang et al. (2024) took a different approach from most dental AI studies by using an ensemble of deep learning models rather than a single model for caries detection. By combining multiple detectors, they were able to reduce false positives while keeping sensitivity high, which is especially important in a clinical setting where unnecessary alarms can make dentists lose trust in the system. Their work confirmed that photographic intraoral images, rather than radiographs, are a sufficient and practical input for reliable AI-based detection, validated against annotations made by experienced dentists. This finding supports the still-image, standard-camera approach used in Toothwise, and gave the team confidence that deep learning for photographic dental screening is not just theoretically possible but has already been shown to work efficiently in a clinical environment similar to CodiSmile Dental Clinic. 
P **AI-Assisted Detection of Interproximal, Occlusal, and Secondary Caries on Bite-Wing Radiographs Using YOLOv8.** Karakus et al. (2024) applied YOLOv8 to the task of detecting different types of dental caries from bitewing radiographs. One thing that stood out in their findings was how well YOLOv8 handled small and overlapping lesions, which is a tricky problem in dental imaging because caries can be close together or partially hidden. Even though Toothwise uses standard photographic images rather than radiographs, the core detection challenge is similar. The model needs to find and correctly label multiple small, carious areas within a single image. The performance of YOLOv8 in this study, combined with the architectural improvements introduced in YOLOv12, provided the team with a strong technical basis for believing that YOLOv12 can handle the multi-condition detection demands of Toothwise. 
P **YOLOv12: Attention-Centric Real-Time Object Detectors.** Tian et al. (2025) introduced YOLOv12 in a preprint that gained significant attention in the computer vision community. The paper presents a new approach to the YOLO architecture centered around area attention mechanisms, which allow the model to focus on relevant regions of an image rather than processing everything equally. The results were impressive. The YOLOv12 N configuration achieved 40.6% mean average precision at an inference latency of just 1.64 milliseconds on a T4 GPU, outperforming comparable YOLOv10 and YOLOv11 models. For a system like Toothwise, where detection results need to be produced quickly enough to fit within a standard dental consultation, that combination of accuracy and speed is exactly what is needed. This paper is the primary technical reference for the detection model used in this project, and its findings directly justify choosing YOLOv12 for evaluation against YOLOv8 for deployment at CodiSmile Dental Clinic. At the time of this review, no widely published study had applied YOLOv12 specifically to multi-condition dental screening from standard photographic images, positioning Toothwise among the earliest efforts to explore this architecture in that context.
P **Feedback Collection and Nearest-Neighbor Profiling for Recommendation Systems in Healthcare Scenarios.** António et al. (2024) developed a physical activity recommendation system for patients in rehabilitation using a K-Nearest Neighbors sampling strategy combined with feedback collection, generating
P activity recommendations by identifying patients with the highest similarity to a given case based on their health profile. Their system was shown to outperform traditional collaborative filtering and purely human-constrained recommendation methods in producing recommendations suited to a patient's specific therapeutic needs, physical capacity, and stage of recovery. This is one of the few studies to apply a KNN-based approach directly to generating personalized, condition-specific health guidance rather than general product or content recommendations, and it provides a close technical parallel for how Toothwise's KNN-based feature classifies a screening into a risk tier and composes a prevention plan by matching a patient's findings against similar synthetic and dentist-confirmed cases.
P **Bridging the Gap or Widening the Divide: A Call for Capacity-Building in Artificial Intelligence for Healthcare in the Philippines.** Gutierrez and Viacrusis (2023) took a critical look at AI readiness in Philippine healthcare. When the researchers tested AI generated responses to clinical queries relevant to Filipino patients, they found notable inaccuracies, including instances where the AI produced fabricated references and incorrect medical guidance. The paper used this to argue that Filipino healthcare professionals urgently need better AI literacy and that unsupervised use of AI clinical tools carries real risks. The call to action was for structured local validation and, most importantly, mandatory human oversight in any clinical AI application. This study speaks directly to a design decision in Toothwise. The system does not allow AI detection results to be shown to patients without going through a dentist first. At CodiSmile Dental Clinic, the dentist reviews every output before it becomes part of the clinical record or is communicated to the patient, which is exactly the kind of human oversight that Gutierrez and Viacrusis argued for.
H 2.3 Synthesis
P The literature and studies reviewed in this chapter come together to build a clear and well-supported case for the approach that Toothwise has taken. Schwendicke et al. (2021) and Mohammad Rahimi et al. (2022) established through separate systematic reviews that deep learning models are genuinely reliable for dental caries detection, often performing at the level of trained clinicians or better. Moharrami et al. (2025) and Zhang (2024) extended this evidence beyond caries alone, showing that plaque, calculus, and gingivitis can also be reliably detected from standard intraoral photographs, with Zhang's work in particular demonstrating that multiple conditions can be detected from a single image source using one model, directly supporting the five-class detection approach used in Toothwise. Kang et al. (2024) and Askar et al. (2021) further confirmed that photographic images captured without specialized intraoral camera hardware are a sufficient and practical input modality for AI-based detection in an actual clinical setting, closely paralleling the still-image, standard-camera acquisition approach of Toothwise at CodiSmile Dental Clinic.
P The most important gap identified across the reviewed works is that existing dental AI systems detect conditions but stop there; the photograph-based reviews by Moharrami et al. (2024, 2025) cover detection only. None of the detection-focused studies reviewed generated recommendations or patient-facing guidance after detection. At the same time, Yue et al. (2021) and António et al. (2024) show that KNN-based recommendation approaches have been successfully applied in other healthcare domains, such as physical rehabilitation, but remain comparatively unexplored in dental screening specifically. Toothwise fills this gap by embedding a KNN-based risk classification and recommendation engine into a multi-condition detection pipeline, pairing YOLO-based detection with an actionable, personalized prevention plan, a combination not found together in the literature reviewed.
P On the technical side, Tian et al. (2025) established YOLOv12 as a highly accurate and efficient real-time object detection architecture, while Jocher et al. (2023), de Brito et al. (2025), Ramírez-Pedraza et al. (2025), and Karakus et al. (2024) documented the strong and well-validated performance of YOLOv8 specifically across caries and plaque detection tasks, supporting its selection as this study's comparison baseline. Askar et al. (2021) further showed that deep learning-based detection is achievable even under limited dataset conditions, supporting the training strategy used in this project.
P The Philippine context literature from the WHO (2022) and Oliveros Villarico et al. (2023) grounds all of this in a concrete public health reality. Dental conditions are widespread among Filipinos, they often go undetected until they have already progressed, and access to professional dental care remains out of reach for many due to cost and geography. Alibudbud et al. (2025) and Gutierrez and Viacrusis (2023) shaped responsible design decisions around human oversight, data privacy, and clinical transparency. What no existing work reviewed has done is bring together YOLO-based multi-condition detection from standard photographic images, a KNN-based risk classification and personalized prevention plan generator, and a full-stack clinical system deployed at an actual Philippine dental clinic. That is the specific contribution of Toothwise.

H 3.1 Development Methodology
P Toothwise was developed using the Agile Software Development Life Cycle (Agile SDLC). Agile was selected because the system integrates several interconnected components, including a React.js web interface, a FastAPI REST backend, a PostgreSQL database, a dual-model YOLOv8/YOLOv12 detection pipeline, a KNN-based risk classification and recommendation engine, authentication and authorization, audit logging, and a separate patient self-service portal. These components benefit from incremental development, continuous testing, regular review, and repeated refinement rather than a single sequential build.
P Instead of completing the entire system in one long development cycle, the project was carried out through manageable iterations in which requirements were prioritized, features were implemented, tested, reviewed, and improved before the next set of features was developed. Each iteration followed the same general cycle of planning, requirements analysis, system design, development, testing, evaluation, and refinement, illustrated in Figure 1.
P Planning Requirements Analysis System Design next Development iteration Testing Evaluation Refinement

P Two cautions apply to these counts. A correct detection on such an image is counted as a false alarm, so the false-alarm counts, and therefore the accuracy, are conservative. Second, the counts depend on the confidence threshold: a lower threshold finds more findings but raises more false alarms.
P For a screening support tool, missed findings are the more serious error because a false alarm is reviewed and dismissed by the dentist, whereas a missed finding may not be examined. This supports keeping the dentist's visual examination as the reference and presenting the detections as suggestions.

H 4.1.7 Ensemble Fusion

Boxes from both models are grouped when they share a label and overlap with an IoU of at least 0.50; each group is merged into one box whose coordinates are the confidence-weighted average of its members (weighted box fusion). A finding confirmed by both models receives a confidence increase of 0.08, capped at 0.98, because agreement between two independently trained models is stronger evidence than either model alone.

P In answer to the first problem, both models learned to detect the five target classes. H 4.2 Specialization and Technical Results
P This section presents the technical results of the implemented system: the detection pipeline, the risk scoring engine, the KNN recommendation feature, the results interface and patient records, role-based access, the patient-facing chatbot, and the measured quality of the web application.
H 4.2.1 AI Detection Pipeline
P The FastAPI backend loads both trained checkpoints when the server starts and exposes the screening workflow through authenticated REST endpoints. For each uploaded intraoral photograph the pipeline (1) validates the file type and size (maximum 15 MB), (2) runs YOLOv8 and YOLOv12, (3) fuses their detections as described in Section 4.1.6, (4) computes a risk score and risk tier, (5) generates a prevention plan, and (6) stores the image, every detection with its bounding box and confidence, the risk result, and the plan in the patient's record. The response includes the fused detections and the raw output of each model.

P The risk score is rule-based so that every score can be explained to the patient and audited by the dentist. Each non-healthy detection contributes its class severity multiplied by its confidence; the score is the highest such value plus 6 points for every additional finding (up to 5 additional findings), capped at 100. H 4.3.4 Statistical Treatment
P The weighted mean of each item is computed as WM = Σ(f × x) / N, where f is the number of respondents who chose a rating, x is the rating (1 to 5), and N is the number of respondents. The mean of a characteristic is the average of its item means, and the overall mean is the average of the six characteristic means. The standard deviation is reported to show how much the respondents agreed with one another.
